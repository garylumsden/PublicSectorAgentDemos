# Dossier: Shared Identity Verification Gateway

> **Entirely fictional demonstration material.** Every organisation, person, service, figure, and event in this Dossier is invented. It does not describe or resemble a real department, programme, public body, or supplier.

## Submission

**Proposal:** Introduce one identity verification gateway for twelve fictional public services.

**Sponsor:** Digital Access Federation (fictional)

**Named accountable officer:** Priya North, Chief Digital Access Officer (fictional)

**Decision sought after assurance:** Permission to make the gateway the default route for new online applications.

## Current service

The twelve services use nine identity products and four manual checking teams. People often repeat document checks when they use a different service. Average completion ranges from 7 minutes to 11 days.

About 18% of users cannot complete the first digital route. Common causes include expired documents, recent address changes, limited credit history, name differences, inaccessible capture steps, and devices without a suitable camera.

Each service offers a manual alternative, but waiting times and evidence rules differ. Three services require an applicant to restart the whole application after a failed identity check.

## Proposed gateway

The proposed **OneProof** gateway will combine document validation, account history, device signals, address data, and optional facial comparison. It will return one of three results:

- verified;
- additional evidence required; or
- manual review required.

The gateway will not make the service eligibility decision. A service may refuse to accept an online application until identity is verified. The programme describes this as a channel control rather than a decision about rights or access.

A common identity record will be retained so a successful check can be reused for 24 months. Services can request a fresh check when risk indicators change. The proposal does not define which service can amend the common record or resolve conflicting identity evidence.

## Evidence and inclusion

A trial used 94,000 volunteers from three services. It completed 87% of checks without staff action. Completion fell to 69% for users aged over 75 and 61% for users without a current passport or driving licence.

The facial comparison supplier reports an overall false rejection rate of 1.7%. The evaluation data is not large enough to provide reliable rates for several demographic groups. The supplier will permit annual independent testing but will not provide its training data or full model details.

Assisted-digital users were tested in staffed offices. The default operating model expects most support through telephone and video calls. No end-to-end accessibility test has covered screen readers, speech input, intermittent connectivity, or a user assisted by a trusted representative.

## Legal authority and accountability

Each service has a legal basis to establish identity for its own function. The programme has not shown that every service can contribute identity evidence to a reusable cross-service record.

The gateway operator will be a processor for transaction checks. Its proposed role for the common record may make it a joint controller. The participating bodies have not agreed this point.

The service owner will set technical policy. Each public body will decide when identity assurance is sufficient. The proposal does not assign accountability when the gateway result is technically correct but unsuitable for a service's legal threshold.

## Cost and benefits

The five-year cost is GBP 73 million. Claimed benefits are GBP 126 million:

| Benefit | Estimate |
| --- | ---: |
| Retire existing verification contracts | GBP 42 million |
| Reuse completed checks | GBP 39 million |
| Reduce manual checking | GBP 31 million |
| Reduce identity fraud | GBP 14 million |

The model assumes 72% of successful checks will be reused. Current research indicates that 44% of users are willing to reuse a check across services. The estimate excludes the cost of maintaining manual routes during migration.

## Security and resilience

OneProof will be a common entry point for twelve services. A compromise could expose identity evidence or prevent access across government.

The design uses separate service connections, encrypted evidence, hardware-protected keys, and fraud monitoring. It retains biometric comparison results for 30 days and other identity evidence for 24 months. Security has requested a shorter retention period and independent penetration testing before live facial comparison.

The recovery target is four hours. Four participating services have statutory deadlines shorter than one working day. Their fallback process relies on local staff who are scheduled to transfer to the shared operator.

## User and staff evidence

Users support avoiding repeated checks but want a clear choice about reuse. They expect a non-digital route that does not delay access. Several participants believed a successful check would let every public body see the documents they supplied.

Operational staff support one technical service but want discretion to accept alternative evidence. Fraud teams want richer device and account signals. Inclusion teams warn that those signals can penalise people with unstable housing, shared devices, or limited financial records.

## Open matters for assessment

The accountable officer asks the Board to assess whether OneProof should become the default route, whether facial comparison should enter the first release, how consent and legal authority apply to reuse, what manual route is required, and which resilience and inclusion conditions must precede approval.
