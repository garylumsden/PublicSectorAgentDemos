# Dossier: Cross-Government Emergency Housing Triage

> **Entirely fictional demonstration material.** Every organisation, person, service, figure, and event in this Dossier is invented. It does not describe or resemble a real department, programme, public body, or supplier.

## Submission

**Proposal:** Create a shared triage service for emergency housing applications across six fictional local authorities and one national support agency.

**Sponsor:** Emergency Accommodation Partnership (fictional)

**Named accountable officer:** Amina Calder, Director for Housing Resilience (fictional)

**Decision sought after assurance:** Permission to begin a nine-month pilot before the winter demand peak.

## Current service

The seven bodies receive about 185,000 requests for emergency accommodation each year. Each body uses a different assessment form and priority scheme. People who move between areas often repeat sensitive information about health, disability, family safety, debt, and immigration status.

Call handlers make urgent placement decisions under time pressure. The current median decision time is 19 hours. During severe weather, the median rises to 41 hours. Three authorities report that they cannot consistently provide interpreters outside office hours.

## Proposed service

The proposed **SafeNight** platform will create one intake form and a shared triage model. The model will summarise evidence, recommend a priority band, identify missing documents, and suggest the authority responsible for the case.

A housing officer will make the final placement decision. The proposal permits automatic low-risk routing when the model reports confidence above 92%. It does not define how confidence will be calibrated across different groups or how staff will identify a confident but incorrect recommendation.

The national support agency will operate the platform. Each authority will keep its statutory duties and funding responsibility. The proposal does not identify who is accountable when the platform sends a person to the wrong authority or gives a low priority to an urgent case.

## Evidence and equality

A prototype used 48,000 historical applications from two authorities. It reduced average handling time by 28%. The evaluation excluded applications made through interpreters, cases with no fixed address, and most records involving domestic abuse.

The prototype produced a higher urgent-priority rate for applicants with complete digital evidence. Applicants using telephone or assisted-digital routes were more likely to receive a request for more information. The programme describes this as a data-quality issue. The Equality Lead states that the difference can create indirect discrimination.

The training data records sex but not gender identity. Disability information is held as free text. Ethnicity data is missing in 31% of records. The proposal does not define a minimum evidence standard for the pilot.

## Delivery and cost

The three-year cost is estimated at GBP 29 million. The business case claims GBP 44 million in savings from fewer repeat assessments, shorter temporary accommodation stays, and a reduction of 210 administrative posts.

The delivery schedule allows 14 weeks for integration with 23 local systems. Five systems have no supported application programming interface. The supplier proposes screen automation for those systems during the pilot.

Operations recommends a manual review of every priority recommendation for the first six months. Finance states that this would remove GBP 8 million from the savings case. The programme board has not resolved the difference.

## Information use

SafeNight will retain the shared intake record for seven years. Current local retention ranges from two to twelve years. The proposed record will be available to all participating housing teams after a person moves between areas.

The legal analysis supports sharing data required to prevent immediate harm. It does not establish a basis for routine access to past domestic abuse, immigration, or health information after the emergency has ended. The correction process requires each authority to update its own source record before the shared record changes.

## Security and resilience

The service target is 99.9% availability. The proposed fallback is a shared spreadsheet distributed by secure email. No live exercise has tested this fallback.

The platform will hold current locations for people at risk. Privileged support staff can search all records. The design includes role-based access and audit logs, but no alert for unusual bulk searches. A supplier support team outside the participating bodies will have time-limited production access.

## User and staff evidence

Research with 68 applicants supports a single intake process. Participants strongly opposed repeating traumatic details. They also wanted a person to review any decision that could leave them without accommodation.

Front-line staff support faster evidence summaries. They are concerned that performance targets will make the recommended priority band difficult to challenge. Local safeguarding teams want access to the full shared record. Information Rights recommends a smaller emergency evidence view.

## Open matters for assessment

The accountable officer asks the Board to assess whether the pilot should proceed, whether automatic routing is acceptable, what equality evidence is required, which data should be shared, what fallback must be tested, and which unresolved risks require explicit acceptance.
